# Israeli Municipal Internal Audit Report

## Legal notice

This is a free information tool operated by an AI model. It explains the statutory framework for the internal audit of an Israeli local authority and helps you structure your own report. All of its output is produced automatically by an AI model, with no involvement, review or approval by an advocate or a certified internal auditor. The output is not legal advice and not a legal opinion, only a general explanation: it does not read your working papers, does not check current case law or ministry circulars, and does not weigh the circumstances of your authority. An AI model can err, omit data, or present a wrong conclusion.

The report you sign is your own professional work product and your own responsibility. Any wording this tool suggests is raw material for you to write from, not a finished report, and it is not a substitute for advice that takes account of the particular data and needs of each person. Note in particular that the publication of an audit report is restricted by statute and that a premature disclosure is an offence, so verify the permitted timing before circulating any part of it.

## Problem

The internal auditor of an Israeli local authority works to a statutory timetable that is not written down in one place. The report is due by a fixed calendar date, then passes through the mayor, the audit committee and the council on three further clocks, each with its own fallback if the previous actor misses theirs. Getting the chain wrong either delays the council discussion past its statutory limit or, worse, results in disclosure before the report may lawfully be published.

## Instructions

### Step 0: Establish which authority type you are, because the rules are not identical

The Municipalities Ordinance is written for a municipality (iriya). For a local council and a regional council the audit sections apply only through section 13E of the Local Councils Ordinance, which imports a NAMED SUBSET, mutatis mutandis: 167(b) to (d) (appointment and eligibility), 167A, 170A(a) to (d), 170B, 170C, 170C1 and 334A (the publication offence). The deficiency-correction team comes separately, through section 13D, and the audit committee section 149C through section 13B. Several differences change real answers:

| Provision | Municipality | Local council | Regional council |
|---|---|---|---|
| 170A(a) to (d), duties and work plan | Applies | Applies | Applies |
| 170A(e) to (f), the budget-proposal floor, and the 2018 staffing and budget regulations | Applies | **Not imported**, the founding order governs instead | **Not imported** |
| 170B, 170C, 170C1, the timeline and the evidence rule | Applies | Applies | Applies |
| 170C1A, the deficiency-correction team | Applies | Applies (via 13D) | **Excluded by name** |
| 170(e) to (g) office staff, 171 dismissal protection, 166(aa) quarterly committee meetings | Applies | **Not imported**, check the founding order and the applicable Local Councils Order, where they provide | **Not imported** |

Two further points apply only outside a municipality. The minister may provide in the founding order that a local council auditor serves **part time**, having regard to the number of residents and settlements and the size of the budget. Such an auditor may complete the post as public-complaints or employee-complaints commissioner (council approval on the audit committee's recommendation), or by outside work approved by both the council and the minister on four conditions, among them no conflict of interest.

Separately, in every local authority the council must by default appoint the auditor as **public-complaints commissioner** under the Local Authorities (Public Complaints Commissioner) Law 2008, unless it appoints another senior employee for special reasons. That role has its own annual report to the mayor and the council, due **1 May**, which the council discusses within two months and which is published on the authority's website. Keep the two calendars apart. Information from complaints may be used for the audit role (section 4 of that Law allows it), but the evidentiary rules differ: complaints documents are inadmissible even in disciplinary proceedings.

For a municipality, the minimum staff and budget of the auditor's office are set by regulations; the full tables are in `references/auditor-office-standards.md`.

Settle this first. Everything below is stated for a municipality unless flagged.

### Step 1: Confirm what is in scope for the audit

Under section 170A(a) the auditor examines whether the authority's actions, including actions under the Planning and Building Law, were lawful, taken by the person authorised to take them, and taken while preserving purity of morals (tohar hamidot) and the principles of efficiency and economy. The role also covers the actions of the authority's employees, whether the internal control arrangements and procedures secure compliance with every law, and the bookkeeping together with the safekeeping of the authority's funds and property.

The audit is not confined to the authority itself. It extends to the religious council in the authority's area, and to any corporation, enterprise, institution, fund or body in whose annual budget the authority participates by more than one tenth for that budget year, or in whose management appointment it participates. Anything caught by that test is an "audited municipal body" (guf ironi mevukar). The one-tenth test is a common source of scope arguments, so establish it per budget year rather than assuming last year's answer still holds.

One carve-out: a water and sewage corporation. Section 138 of the Water and Sewage Corporations Law disapplies the Municipalities Ordinance to such a company (the consolidated Ordinance text notes that 170A(b) does not apply to it), except a sewage company that is a city association. While a local authority controls it, the company has its own internal auditor under the Internal Audit Law, who sends copies of the audit reports to the Government Water and Sewage Authority and its commissioner for water corporations. The carve-out removes the company as an audited body; the municipality's own actions as shareholder remain actions of the municipality under 170A(a).

Elected council members are not outside the audit. The Supreme Court held in HCJ 7805/00 (Aloni) that the internal audit may examine an elected official's acts insofar as they relate to the municipality's actions, including purity of morals and conflicts of interest, but not political activity as such.

### Step 2: Set the work plan against the three lawful sources

Section 170A(c) gives exactly three sources for the annual work plan, the audit topics for a given period and the audit scope:
1. The auditor's own discretion.
2. A demand by the mayor to audit a particular matter.
3. A demand by the audit committee, and this one is capped at **two topics per work year**.

The cap matters. A committee that sends a longer list is not entitled to have all of it taken up as of right, and the auditor sets the method of carrying out the audit at their own discretion under 170A(d).

Use the access powers in section 170B while doing the work. The mayor and deputies, council members, employees, the religious council and the members and employees of every audited municipal body must deliver any document the auditor considers necessary and any information or explanation requested, within the period and in the manner the demand sets. The auditor has access to every database and data-processing program of the authority and of audited municipal bodies, and must be invited to every meeting of the council and its committees. A refusal is itself a finding: record it in the report as a limitation on the audit's scope.

### Step 3: Write the report to the three statutory contents

The statute names what the report must contain. Structure it that way, because the committee and the council read it against these headings:
- A summary of the auditor's actions over the year.
- A detailing of the deficiencies (likuyim) found.
- Recommendations for correcting those deficiencies and preventing their recurrence in future.

Recommendations that do not connect to a specific detailed deficiency, and deficiencies with no recommendation attached, are the two most common structural weaknesses in a municipal report.

**Before the report is final, give the audited parties a hearing.** The Ordinance says nothing about this, but HCJ 7805/00 (Aloni) holds that the right to be heard exists anyway, and that where a person's good name is at stake the auditee's right of inspection generally requires handing over all the material relevant to the audit, unless reasons of special importance rooted in the audit function justify withholding it (sources may be protected). In that case the report was defective because the final version contained allegations and facts the petitioner had never been given a full chance to answer, and material documents had not been shown to her. The court did not void the report: it held that such defects do not necessarily invalidate it from the outset, and returned it to the auditor to hand over the remaining documents (identifying details of sources may be removed and internal working memos kept privileged) and to allow a further response. So circulate drafts, and do not add new allegations to the final version without sending them back for response.

### Step 4: Work the timeline, including both fallback branches

This is the part that is easy to get wrong, because two different branches lead to two different final deadlines.

**The main chain:**

| Stage | Deadline | Trigger |
|---|---|---|
| Auditor submits the annual report to the mayor | Not later than **1 April** of the year following the audited year | Calendar |
| Auditor delivers a copy to the audit committee | Same time as submission to the mayor | Submission |
| Mayor submits comments to the committee, and gives every council member a copy of the report with the comments | **3 months** | From the mayor receiving the report |
| Committee submits conclusions and proposals to the council for approval | **2 months** | From receiving the mayor's comments |
| Council holds a special discussion and decides on approval | **2 months** | From the committee's submission |

Each downstream clock runs from the ACTUAL date of the step before it. If the mayor comments after one month, the committee's two months run from then, not from month three. The committee may summon officeholders of the authority or of an audited body to respond before it completes its conclusions.

The audit committee itself (section 149C) has at most seven members, roughly mirroring the council's factions; the mayor, deputies and management-committee members may not sit on it, and its chair comes from the opposition and may not be a director of an audited municipal body. Under section 166(aa) a committee the law requires must meet at least once every three months, and if it does not, the mayor directs that it be convened.

**Fallback branch A, the mayor does not comment in time.** The committee does not wait indefinitely. It discusses the report and submits its conclusions and proposals to the council within **5 months** from the date the auditor delivered the report to the committee.

**Fallback branch B, the committee does not submit, or the mayor does not circulate.** If the committee has not submitted its conclusions to council members by the end of its period, or the mayor has not given every council member a copy with the comments, the auditor delivers a copy of the report to every council member directly, and the council discusses the report and its recommendations **not later than 7 months** from its submission to the mayor.

Where the committee submitted on time but copies were not circulated, a strict reading may still hold the council to two months from the committee's submission; aim for the earlier date. Treating a mayor who commented late as not having circulated in time is the conservative reading.

Note that branch B puts a duty on the auditor. It is the auditor who delivers the report to council members when the other actors have not performed.

The auditor may also submit an additional report to the mayor and the committee at any time they see fit, or when the mayor or the committee demands it. Whether the chain above applies to an additional report is not settled by the text; route it through the same chain, and treat it as closed until then unless publication is permitted under Step 5.

### Step 5: Apply the publication restriction before anything circulates

Section 170C(f) has two limbs. First, no person may publish a report under this section, any part of it, or its contents, before the date set for its submission to the council has passed. Second, no one may publish an audit finding of the municipal auditor, and this limb carries no date on its face. The auditor or the mayor may permit publication, but only with the committee's approval, and a permission may carry conditions.

Breach of the ban, or of a condition in a permission, is an offence under section 334A, punishable by **one year's imprisonment**. Treat this as the first question on any request to share a draft, brief a journalist, or answer a freedom-of-information request touching the report. The restriction covers findings, not merely the bound report.

Three cautions. First, the statute does not define "the date set for its submission to the council", so the conservative course is to treat the report as closed until the LATER of the council's special discussion and the statutory deadline for the committee's submission to the council; if the council never discusses it, seek a permission rather than assuming the ban has lapsed. Second, a finding that is not in the published report (working papers, a draft finding that was dropped) stays under the second limb: do not release it without a permission. Third, early publication by permission is not routine: Aloni describes it as an exception meant for rare cases where there is a first-rank public interest in immediate disclosure, and it held an early publication there to be wrong. The Ordinance's website section (248B) does not list the auditor's report among what must be posted, unlike the public-complaints commissioner's report.

### Step 6: Know the evidentiary status of what you produce

The auditor's reports, opinions, and any other document produced or prepared in the role are **not admissible as evidence in any legal proceeding**. They are, however, expressly not disqualified from serving as evidence in a disciplinary proceeding.

This cuts both ways and is worth stating plainly to anyone who asks the office for material: a finding is not a legal determination and cannot be used as one in court, but it can support discipline.

### Step 7: Route the two matters that leave the ordinary chain

- **Suspected offence involving the mayor or the legal advisor.** Where the auditor has grounds to believe that the mayor or the authority's legal advisor is party to an offence under Chapter E, Article B of the Penal Law, the matter goes **directly to the State Comptroller**, not through the mayor or the committee. Do not label this "bribery only": in the current Penal Law, Chapter E Article B is headed "parties to an offence", while the bribery offences sit in Chapter 9 Article 5. Which offences the cross-reference captures is a question for counsel; when in doubt, treat a suspected criminal offence by either officeholder as a candidate for the direct route.
- **Correcting deficiencies.** A deficiency-correction team appointed under section 21A(b) of the State Comptroller Law, headed by the director general, discusses the ways and the dates for correcting deficiencies found in the report once the council has dealt with it, and the ways to prevent recurrence. It submits its recommendations to the mayor **within 3 months** of the council's discussion and reports to the audit committee on implementation **once every 3 months**. The mayor may defer correcting a particular deficiency only by giving written reasons to the auditor and the committee **not later than 3 months** after receiving the team's recommendations. **This does not apply to a regional council**, which is excluded from section 170C1A by name; a regional council still needs the section 21A team for State Comptroller reports.
- **State Comptroller reports run on a different track.** The audit committee must discuss every report of the State Comptroller, the Ombudsman and the Ministry of the Interior on the authority, and follow the correction of deficiencies (149C(a)). For a State Comptroller report, the mayor submits comments to the Comptroller within **ten weeks** of receiving it; the correction team discusses the deficiencies within **sixty days** of the date the report is tabled in the Knesset and reports its decisions to the mayor within **15 days**; the mayor reports to the Comptroller within **thirty days** (sections 20(d), 21A(c), 21B(a)). Failing these duties without reasonable justification is a disciplinary offence (21C). Publishing a State Comptroller report or its contents before it is tabled is itself punishable by one year's imprisonment (28(a)(1)). Do not mix these dates with the 170C chain.

### Step 8: Produce the dated schedule

Close by giving the user their actual dates: the 1 April submission, each downstream deadline on the main chain, both fallback dates alongside, the correction-team dates, and the conservative publication date. `scripts/audit_timeline.py --audited-year YYYY` computes them; pass `--submitted`, `--mayor-comments`, `--committee-submitted` and `--council-discussed` as those events happen, because every later clock runs from the actual earlier date. Add `--not-circulated` if the mayor did not give council members copies with the comments (fallback B), and `--regional-council` for a regional council. Without them it shows the latest lawful dates. On a host that cannot run scripts, compute the same chain by hand from the table in Step 4.

## Gotchas

Agent failure modes specific to this domain:

- **Collapsing the two fallback branches.** There are two different end points, 5 months and 7 months, measured from two different events. The 5-month clock runs from the auditor's delivery to the committee; the 7-month clock runs from submission to the mayor. Quoting one for the other misstates the deadline.
- **Treating the report as publishable once submitted.** It is not. The report limb runs until the date set for submission to the council; the findings limb has no date on its face, so a finding outside the published report still needs a permission.
- **Describing a finding as a legal determination.** The statute makes these documents inadmissible in legal proceedings while expressly preserving them for disciplinary ones. Both halves matter.
- **Assuming the audit stops at the authority's own departments.** The more-than-one-tenth budget test and the management-appointment test pull in corporations, funds and institutions, and the answer is per budget year.
- **Accepting an unlimited committee work-plan list.** The committee's demand is capped at two topics per work year.
- **Confusing this role with the public-complaints role or with the State Comptroller.** They are separate statutory functions even though the auditor is the default public-complaints commissioner. The complaints report is due 1 May and is published online; its documents are inadmissible even in disciplinary proceedings.
- **Assuming the Internal Audit Law 1992 binds a municipality.** Its definition of a public body does not include a local authority, so its duties do not apply by statute; it is referenced only for experience equivalence in eligibility. A religious council is a public body under it.
- **Skipping the hearing.** A report that names people and adds allegations they never answered is defective and may be returned for a further hearing and correction (Aloni).
- **Forgetting the auditor's protection.** In a municipality, an auditor who is an employee may be dismissed only by a three-quarters majority of council members, after being heard (171(b)(2), (c)). This section is not imported for local councils, where the founding order governs dismissal.
- **Applying the municipality rules wholesale to a local or regional council.** Only a named subset is imported. The budget-proposal floor is not among it, and the deficiency-correction team excludes regional councils by name.

## Troubleshooting

| Symptom | Likely cause | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| The mayor has not returned comments and the committee is idle | The 3-month period lapsed and nobody moved to the fallback | The committee's own clock is 5 months from the auditor's delivery to it. It does not need the mayor's comments to act |
| Council members say they never received the report | The mayor did not circulate it with the comments | This triggers the branch where the auditor delivers copies directly, and the council must discuss within 7 months of submission to the mayor |
| A request arrives to share the report before the council discussion | Publication restriction not considered | Publication before the date set for submission to the council is prohibited. Permission requires the auditor or the mayor acting with the committee's approval |
| A department claims it is outside the audit | The one-tenth test was not applied for the current budget year | Establish the participation share for that budget year, and check the separate management-appointment limb |
| Someone wants to use a finding in litigation | Evidentiary status misunderstood | These documents are not admissible in legal proceedings, though they are available in disciplinary ones |
| An audited official says they never saw the allegations in the final report | Allegations were added after the last draft they answered | Send the new material back for response before finalising; Aloni treated this as a defect |
| A department or corporation will not hand over documents or system access | The 170B duties were not invoked in writing | Make a written demand setting the period and manner; if it is still refused, record the refusal as a scope limitation in the report |
